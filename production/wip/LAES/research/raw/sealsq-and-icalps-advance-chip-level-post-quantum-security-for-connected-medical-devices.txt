SEALSQ and IC'Alps Advance Chip-Level Post-Quantum Security for Connected Medical Devices
Post-quantum secure elements, Trusted Platform Modules and custom ultra-low-power ASICs address the IoMT security readiness gap, with live medical demonstrations at Electronica 2026.
-- SEALSQ Corp (NASDAQ: LAES) ("SEALSQ" or "Company"), a company that focuses on developing and selling Semiconductors, PKI, and Post-Quantum technology hardware and software products, today outlined how SEALSQ and its subsidiary IC'Alps can help manufacturers build post-quantum cryptography (PQC) into next-generation Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) devices at the chip level.
The initiative addresses a significant post-quantum readiness gap in connected healthcare.

The findings highlight the importance of incorporating post-quantum security into new medical-device designs, particularly as many existing devices have long operating lifecycles and limited upgrade options.
A Readiness Gap Where Patient Care Depends on It
Forescout analyzed more than 2.5 million devices across more than 50 healthcare delivery organizations. Its findings show that the devices hardest to upgrade are often the ones hospitals rely on most for patient care:
6% of IoMT devices and 16% of operational technology devices use SSH implementations that support PQC, against 50% of IT devices.
Forescout identified more than 5,500 internet-exposed medical information systems. On average, 31% use TLS 1.3, the only TLS version that supports standardized PQC.
TLS 1.3 use stands at 33% for electronic medical record systems, 36% for picture archiving systems and 13% for laboratory management systems.
The risk is immediate because attackers can harvest encrypted data now and decrypt it once quantum computers are powerful enough. Forescout notes that patient data stays valuable for a lifetime and cannot be reset like a payment card. The report also frames PQC as a patient-safety issue. Cryptography protects commands sent to devices, device telemetry, medication orders and firmware signatures.
Forescout classes regulated medical devices as low-agility assets, where a cryptographic change can depend on vendor release cycles, recertification or even hardware replacement. That makes device design the most effective point at which to act. Medical devices can remain in service for 10 to 20 years, so a design started today must be ready for the cryptography of the next two decades.
"Patient data stays sensitive for a lifetime, and a medical device can stay in service for many years," said Carlos Moreira, Founder, Chairman and CEO of SEALSQ. "That is why post-quantum security has to be designed in at the chip stage, not added once a device is on the market. The Forescout findings show how wide the gap is today. Together, SEALSQ and IC'Alps give manufacturers a practical way to close it in the devices they design next."
“Our experience designing ASICs for active implantable medical devices has taught us to address patient safety, reliable operation and long battery life together, from the earliest design decisions,” said Jean-Luc Triouleyre, Managing Director and Co-Founder of IC'Alps. “We bring that track record in ultra-low-power design and power management to QASIC, integrating SEALSQ’s post-quantum security alongside sensing and processing while addressing the stringent energy constraints of implants, within the ISO 13485 quality framework our medical customers rely on.”
From Standard Secure Chips to Custom Medical ASICs
SEALSQ and IC'Alps give manufacturers two routes: standard post-quantum chips designed into a product, or a custom ASIC with post-quantum security built in. Both implement the NIST-standardized ML-KEM (FIPS 203) and ML-DSA (FIPS 204) algorithms.

IC'Alps brings the medical expertise that PQC alone cannot supply. Its ISO 13485-certified teams develop custom circuits for smart ultrasound probes, implantable cardiac devices, Holter monitors, intracranial pressure monitoring and brain-computer interfaces. IC’Alps also supports its customers along with the ISO 14971 specific risk management of their product.
That experience matters because post-quantum keys and signatures are larger than those of the cryptography they replace, while implants and wearables have strict limits on power and size. QASIC addresses these constraints at the semiconductor-design stage, when security can be designed alongside sensing, processing, safety and power management.
SEALSQ's healthcare work also reaches the clinician workstation. In September 2026, iMTC Technology selected the QVault TPM183 for its Fingerprint Recognition Medical Mouse, giving the product a hardware root of trust and a migration path to PQC.
Beyond chip-level security, SEALSQ offers Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) through its INeS PKI solution, enabling the issue of digital certificates to every element – the medical device, the physician base station, the cloud, the embedded application, the building network – and the certificate that signs every firmware. Provisioned at manufacturing and managed over the whole lifecycle of the device, these quantum-safe identities secure data connections and software updates, and ultimately patient data and life.
These technologies are intended for new and next-generation products. Devices already in service will continue to need the measures Forescout recommends, including network segmentation, TLS 1.3 enforcement and PQC gateways.
